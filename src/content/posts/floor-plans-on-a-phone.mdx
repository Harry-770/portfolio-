Every real-estate project I've worked on gets its floor plans the same way: a scanned architect's drawing, full of wall hatching, furniture blocks and tiny room labels. It looks great on A3 paper. On a phone it's a grey smudge you have to pinch and drag around.

### Plans are drawn for paper

An architect's drawing answers an architect's questions: wall thickness, column positions, plumbing shafts. A buyer has three questions. How big is each room? Where does the light come from? Will my furniture fit? The original drawing answers all three, but buries them under everything else.

### What changed

I redraw every unit type as a clean plan. Walls are the heaviest line, doors show their swing, windows are breaks in the wall. Room names and sizes are the biggest text on the drawing, in metres with square feet a tap away. There's a north arrow on every plan, because "east facing" is the first thing many families ask about.

<Figure diagram="floorplan" caption="A 2 BHK redrawn for a phone: room sizes as the biggest text, doors with their swing, windows as breaks in the wall, and a north arrow." />

The living room gets a soft tint, because it's the room people imagine themselves in first. The balcony is dashed, because it's outside the carpet area and buyers deserve to know that before they visit, not after.

### Stacking plans for sales teams

The same rule worked for the sales team, just at a bigger scale. Instead of a spreadsheet of unit numbers, they see the whole tower: every floor, every unit, coloured by booked, on hold or available. A broker can tell a buyer what's left on the higher floors in one glance, without calling the office.

<Figure diagram="stacking" caption="Tower B as a stacking plan: twelve floors of four units, coloured by sales status, with totals on the side." />

Plans are information design, not decoration. Draw them for the screen and the person holding it, and most of the questions answer themselves.

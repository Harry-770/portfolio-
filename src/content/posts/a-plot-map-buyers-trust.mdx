The developer had 30 plots in phase one, a beautiful printed brochure and a spreadsheet that three people edited. Buyers would fall in love with a corner plot on the brochure, drive out for a site visit, and learn it had been booked two days earlier. By the third time that happened to the same family, they bought somewhere else.

### The problem with brochures

A brochure is right on the day it's printed. After that, the only honest source was the spreadsheet, and buyers never saw it. Brokers had their own copies on WhatsApp, a week out of date. So the real problem wasn't the design of the map. It was that nobody could trust it.

### One map, three states

We put the masterplan on a single screen that sales, brokers and buyers all share. Every plot has exactly three states: booked, on hold, or available. A hold lasts 48 hours and then frees itself, so plots stop getting stuck "reserved" for someone who never called back.

<Figure diagram="masterplan" caption="Phase one as one live map: booked plots in solid blue, holds in grey, available plots outlined. Tapping a plot shows its size, facing and road width." />

Tapping a plot shows what buyers asked about first in every call: size, which way it faces, and how wide the road in front is. Price came after. We moved it down the card and calls got shorter, because the first three questions were already answered.

### The buyer's journey

We mapped every step from the first search to the signed agreement, and wrote down what a buyer needs at each one. The gap was obvious once it was drawn: most people dropped off between shortlisting and the site visit, because booking a visit meant a phone call during office hours.

<Figure diagram="journey" caption="Five steps from search to agreement. The site visit lost the most buyers, so it got the most design time: two taps to book a slot, with directions sent straight after." />

The token step mattered too. People are nervous paying for land online, so the refund terms sit right next to the UPI button instead of in a PDF. Nothing hidden, nothing to scroll for.

### What I'd do again

Start with the data, not the map. The masterplan looked finished in a week, but it took a month to get one trustworthy source of availability behind it. That month is the whole project. A pretty map with wrong numbers is worse than no map, because it breaks trust in a way a brochure never could.
